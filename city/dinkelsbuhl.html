Landmarks

Sights in Dinkelsbühl: what I would eat first.

Landmarks are fruit. The bigger the sight, the bigger the bonus.

  • Münster St. Georg 1,500 points

    A hall church tall enough to keep its own weather. Walk round it and read the tracery: 26 windows, no two alike, and one of them is the most appetising thing in town. I have tasted most of the town. I am not saying which one.

  • Deutschordensschloss 500 points

    Four wings around one courtyard, far too fancy for the quiet lane it sits on. Up the stairs, past a coat of arms, something small and frilly as a cream cake is waiting at the top. The people working downstairs pass it every day. I am not telling you what it is.

  • Museum 3. Dimension 500 points

    By the Nördlinger Tor, the town mill dressed itself up as a fortress, and now its front rooms hold pictures that refuse to stay flat. Bring both eyes. I am a rock: I have had three dimensions all along, and nobody built me a museum.

  • St. Paul 500 points

    On the Nördlinger Straße stands a church in sober classical dress, younger than most of the houses around it. The ground under it is much older and knows more. Old ground is my favourite course. What it knows, I am not telling you.

  • Bäuerlinsturm 250 points

    Down by the river, a slim tower wears a wooden hat that sticks out over its walls, like a muffin over the edge of its tin. Somebody kept house up there with two jobs, and one of them smelled. I am not telling you which.

  • Faulturm 250 points

    A tall tower with a pointed hat stands over a pond, admiring itself. Its name sounds like an insult: lazy, or rotten. I have been called worse, and I have been lying here longer. Why it got that name, I am not saying.

  • Schweinemarkt 250 points

    The square still answers to Pig Market, and it has grown far too pretty for the name. I only see cobbles, which for me is a buffet. Somebody else saw a great deal more here. I am not saying who.

  • Segringer Tor 250 points

    This gate wears a domed hat that none of the town's other gates would be seen in. Under the arch, steps go down into the old moat. A gate with a pantry, as far as I am concerned. Why it dresses so differently, I am not telling you.

Eating

What to eat in Dinkelsbühl: actual food.

Dinkelsbühl's own dish swims. The country around the wall is one of Bavaria's big carp regions, and the kitchen is Franconian: dumplings, wood-oven bread, sausages cooked in a sour stock. Carp from September to April, dumplings all year.

Meat and fish

  • Dinkelsbühler Karpfen

    A carp with the town in its name. Early in the 20th century a farming official born in the town raised a fast-growing strain out of the old local breed. It still needs three summers to reach a 1.5 kg table fish. Eat it in the months with an r, September to April, baked or cooked blue. Three summers to grow one lunch.

    Come at the end of October or early November for the town's Fischernte week, with a display on the Marktplatz.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern

  • Blaue Zipfel

    Raw Franconian Bratwürste, cooked in a stock of vinegar, onions and spices. They come out a little sour and bluish, which gives them the name. In Franconia you eat them straight from the stock, with a slice of wood-oven bread. Blue sounds like a warning. It is a recipe.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern

  • Dinkelsbühler Schneckennudel

    The town's festive pastry: about 100 g of dough, rolled so the top looks like a snail shell, mostly eaten with coffee or tea. It belongs to the Kinderzeche in July, when it also comes warm. Some bakeries now sell it all year, others only for about two weeks in July. Dinkelsbühlers who moved away have it posted to them at Kinderzeche time. A snail that travels by post. Faster than the original.

    Ask the bakery what is in the dough. The register does not say, which is why it sits here and not with the vegetarian dishes.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern

Vegetarian

  • Fränkische Mehlklöße

    Dumplings of flour, eggs and salt, from a dough like Swabian Spätzle, only runnier. They come with roasts and vegetables, beans for one, or sweet with preserved fruit such as pears. Leftovers are sliced and fried the next day as Kloßbröckchen. A dumpling with a second life.

    They often sit beside a roast. Order them with vegetables or with the pears.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern

  • Fränkische rohe Klöße (Kniedla)

    Potato dumplings made from raw potatoes, loose or firm depending on the region. Tear one apart before you eat it, so more sauce clings. They used to be called green dumplings, because cold ones turned dark green. So, eat them hot.

    They usually come with a roast and its sauce. Ask for a sauce without meat.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern

  • Fränkisches Holzofenbrot mit Quark

    Franconian wood-oven bread, eaten the traditional way with Quark or weißer Käs, the white cheese. It is what the threshers were given at harvest. For a slice of that, I would have threshed too.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern

Vegan

Two, because the register names only these two Middle Franconian foods as plant only. For the dumplings and pastries it does not say what fat or egg goes in, and I do not guess.

  • Fränkisches Holzofenbrot

    Rye flour, water and salt. The sourdough is led through three or four stages over about 24 hours, then the loaf bakes for over an hour in a wood oven whose floor is the heat: the wood burns right on it and the embers are raked out. Franconia has baked it like this for several hundred years. Three ingredients, one day, one fire. Bread does not get much plainer.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern

  • Merkendorfer Sauerkraut

    Merkendorf in Landkreis Ansbach calls itself the Krautstadt, cabbage town, and has grown cabbage since the Thirty Years' War, about twenty varieties of it. From planting to sauerkraut, 80 percent of the work is done by hand, and the sauerkraut gets only a little salt. Cabbage, salt and a great many hands.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern

Name origin

Why it is called Dinkelsbühl.

Dinkelsbühl is Thingolt's hill: "bühl" is the rolling ground, and Thingolt, says the town's own history, was the royal steward who ran the king's land around it. The Bavarian history house leaves the first half open between a personal name and a court place, a "Ding". On paper the name turns up in 1188 as "burgum tinkelspuhel". The spelt came later, when the arms read the name as grain, and the grain grew a founding legend of its own, kept at St. Paul. I read everything as grain, so I understand.

Sources: Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Stadtgeschichte Teil I; Haus der Bayerischen Geschichte, Bayerns Gemeinden: Große Kreisstadt Dinkelsbühl, Wappen; Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Historical Overview

Why here

Somebody stood here and decided.

On a gentle slope west of the Wörnitz, by a ford. Near the crossing, two great old roads met: the trade and pilgrim road from Würzburg to Augsburg, on its way to Italy, and an east-west road from the Middle Rhine to the Danube. Crossroads make people stop, and people who stop get hungry. I respect that.

Sources: Bayerisches Landesamt für Denkmalpflege, Denkmalliste Dinkelsbühl (Baudenkmäler), Ensemble Altstadt Dinkelsbühl, Nr. E-5-71-136-1, Stand 22.09.2026, p. 1; Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Stadtgeschichte Teil I

Why then

When the first stone went down.

Around 1170/80, when the Staufer emperor Friedrich I, Barbarossa, raised the place to a town, most likely to guard the crossing of two imperial roads at the river. In 1188 it appears on paper as a "burgus", in a marriage contract: Barbarossa hands it to his son Konrad for a planned wedding. A town that starts life as a wedding present. I hope somebody brought cake.

Sources: Bayerisches Landesamt für Denkmalpflege, Denkmalliste Dinkelsbühl (Baudenkmäler), Ensemble Altstadt Dinkelsbühl, Nr. E-5-71-136-1, Stand 22.09.2026, p. 1; Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Stadtgeschichte Teil I

Who ruled

Everyone who thought they owned this.

I outlasted all of them. It is the one thing I am good at.

1188–1274

A Staufer royal town. Barbarossa raised it, and in 1188 it went into his son Konrad's marriage contract. When the Staufer line was gone, King Rudolf I of Habsburg brought Dinkelsbühl back to the Empire in 1274.

Sources: Bayerisches Landesamt für Denkmalpflege, Denkmalliste Dinkelsbühl (Baudenkmäler), Ensemble Altstadt Dinkelsbühl, Nr. E-5-71-136-1, Stand 22.09.2026, p. 1; Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Stadtgeschichte Teil I

1274–1802

A Free Imperial City, answering only to the emperor. It was granted Ulm's town law in 1305, and in 1401 it took the office of the Amtmann, the official set over it, in pledge. In 1387 the craft guilds won their seats on the council, six guilds now governed alongside the old families, and Dinkelsbühl became a little republic of its own. That lasted until 1802. Craftsmen on the council. If any of them baked, they had my vote.

Sources: Bayerisches Landesamt für Denkmalpflege, Denkmalliste Dinkelsbühl (Baudenkmäler), Ensemble Altstadt Dinkelsbühl, Nr. E-5-71-136-1, Stand 22.09.2026, p. 1; Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Stadtgeschichte Teil I; Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Historical Overview

1802–

Bavaria, with a detour. In 1802 the town fell to the Electorate of Bavaria, in 1804 it was swapped to the Prussian principality of Ansbach, and in 1806 Prussia handed it on to the new Kingdom of Bavaria. Passed around like the last sandwich. Then in 1826 King Ludwig I forbade tearing down the town walls and towers, and that is why the ring is still whole.

Sources: Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Stadtgeschichte Teil II; Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Historical Overview; Bayerisches Landesamt für Denkmalpflege, Denkmalliste Dinkelsbühl (Baudenkmäler), Ensemble Altstadt Dinkelsbühl, Nr. E-5-71-136-1, Stand 22.09.2026, p. 3

The emblem

What the shield means.

On red, three golden ears of grain side by side on a silver three-peaked mount. The ears are spelt, Dinkel, and the mount is the hill, so the shield spells out the name. The oldest seals, from 1334 and 1341, tried two ideas: an eagle growing out of a hill between ears of spelt, the eagle standing for the emperor's steward, and the ears alone on the mount, which people first called the citizens' sign. By the 19th century only the small shield with the ears was still in use. The town chose bread over bird. Correct.

Sources: Haus der Bayerischen Geschichte, Bayerns Gemeinden: Große Kreisstadt Dinkelsbühl, Wappen

Emblem: Public domain — official coat of arms (Wikimedia Commons)

Three facts

Three things I know about this place.

  1. The wall ring is all still there: 2,500 metres of it, with four inner gates and 20 wall towers, its outer bailey walls and their towers included. Its curve gives the Old Town the outline of a shoe. The Bavarian monument office counts it, together with Rothenburg's, among the best-kept late medieval town walls in Germany. A shoe made of stone. I tried a bite. Tough.

    Sources: Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Stadtgeschichte Teil I; Bayerisches Landesamt für Denkmalpflege, Denkmalliste Dinkelsbühl (Baudenkmäler), Ensemble Altstadt Dinkelsbühl, Nr. E-5-71-136-1, Stand 22.09.2026, p. 2

  2. The Wörnitztor, the eastern gate, still shows the town's oldest wall: big rough-faced blocks from the late Staufer wall of the 13th century. The tower was raised around 1400 and got its Renaissance gable storey around 1600. Three ages stacked like a layer cake, and the bottom layer is the crunchiest.

    Sources: Bayerisches Landesamt für Denkmalpflege, Denkmalliste Dinkelsbühl (Baudenkmäler), Wörnitztor, Nr. D-5-71-136-18, Stand 22.09.2026, p. 7; Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Stadtgeschichte Teil I

  3. Every year the town holds the Kinderzeche, a children's and home-town festival that grew out of a school feast first recorded in 1629. Its play, performed since 1897, acts out a local legend in which the town's children save Dinkelsbühl in the Thirty Years' War by asking for peace. Since 2016 it has been on Germany's list of intangible cultural heritage. On the Tuesday, every child taking part gets a bag full of sweets. That is the scene I would rehearse.

    Sources: Stadt Dinkelsbühl, Kinderzeche; bavarikon (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek), Inheriting Culture: „Kinderzeche“ historical festival play in Dinkelsbühl

Street words

What the names on the signs mean.

Markt
A market. The old imperial roads were widened for trading, so here a Markt is often a broad street rather than a square, and several are named for what they sold. I read them like a menu.
Gasse
A lane, narrower than a Straße. Inside the wall there are plenty of them. Narrow suits me. I eat lengthwise.
Tor
A gate. Four still stand in the wall: the Rothenburger, Nördlinger and Segringer Tor point down the road to the next place, and the Wörnitztor to the river.
Weiher
A pond. A whole chain of them lies in front of the wall. I do not eat ponds. You walk around them.
Mauerweg
A wall path: the lane that runs along the inside of the town wall. Follow it and the wall keeps you company the whole way.
