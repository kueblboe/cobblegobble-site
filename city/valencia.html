Landmarks

What I would eat first.

Landmarks are fruit. The bigger the sight, the bigger the bonus.

  • Llotja de la Seda 1,500 points

    When silk made this city rich, this is where the money changed hands. Go into the great hall and look at what holds the roof up. I am not telling you what they are doing. Then look at the tower and wonder what it was for. Silk, by the way, is not food. I checked.

  • Catedral de València 500 points

    The Seu has a bell tower called the Micalet, named after its own bell. Once a week, at noon, a meeting gathers at one of its doors, out in the open, whatever the weather. And somewhere inside sits a very famous cup. I am not telling you whose. I asked whether it takes soup. Apparently not.

  • Corts Valencianes 500 points

    Lawmakers argue in here these days, which seems a waste of good stone. Before them it was a 15th-century home, and the household who kept it was famous far beyond this street. I am not telling you how famous. Later it did a humbler job for a while. That part I like best.

  • Torres dels Serrans 500 points

    The big gate on the edge of the old riverbed, where the garden runs now. From the front it is two stone lumps with a mouth between them, which I find very relatable. Walk through to the other side before you decide what it is. The front and the back do not agree. It has also looked after some very valuable guests. I am not telling you which.

  • Basílica de la Mare de Déu dels Desemparats 250 points

    She sits on the square that was once the Roman forum, and the city has a pet name for her. Not a grand one. A fond one. Once a year the square gives her a present so big it needs a crew. I am not telling you what. I can tell you it is not edible, which I hold against it.

  • Església de Sant Nicolau 250 points

    King James I founded the congregation here in 1238. Step inside and look up: the reason to come is over your head, and there is a lot of it. A man who knows ceilings better than most gave it a very big nickname. I am not telling you which. It is the only ceiling I have ever wanted to eat more than the floor.

  • Mercat Central 250 points

    A market hall, open since 1928. By a long way my favourite building in the city. Look at the very top of the domes. Somebody keeps watch up there. I am not telling you who. I once tried to move in here. They noticed.

  • Palau del Marqués de Dosaigües 250 points

    Stand at the front door and give it time. It needs it: every inch is busy. Two stone men on it are hard at work. I am not telling you at what, but the name on the house is a clue. Inside waits a ride grander than most houses. The whole front looks edible. It is not. I asked.

Eating

Actual food.

Valencia cooks from its fields. The rice goes into the paella and the tiger nuts go into the horchata. Plenty of cities make you hunt for one famous plate. Here I just follow the crops.

Meat and fish

One dish, because the paella is the only omnivore plate here with a source strong enough to print. A second joins when a register or three independent publishers back it.

  • Paella valenciana

    In 2021 the regional government declared the paella valenciana cultural heritage. The decree covers more than a recipe: how the rice is grown and harvested, the ways of cooking it, the family lunch on a Sunday, and the know-how handed down. The only heritage site I would finish in one sitting.

    Sources: Generalitat Valenciana

Vegetarian

One dish, because the pumpkin fritters are the only vegetarian plate here that three independent publishers back. I would rather serve one than guess a second.

  • Bunyols de carabassa

    Pumpkin fritters. They are the typical sweet of Fallas week, and they get eaten the rest of the year too. A vegetable went into the fryer and came out as dessert. I call that a career.

    Ask about the dough if you skip egg.

    Sources: ValenciaBonita, Consum, Hule y Mantel

Vegan

One entry, and it is a drink: horchata in its natural recipe is Valencia's own vegan classic, and the only vegan entry three independent publishers back.

  • Orxata de xufa

    Horchata made from tiger nuts. The natural recipe is tiger nuts, water and, if you like, sugar, and that version is vegan. Valencia has been drinking it since at least the 14th century: the first mention of a tiger nut milk turns up in the Llibre de Sent Soví, in 1324. For a stone, that is a quick drink.

    Ask for horchata de chufa by name. A carton that only says horchata may not be the real thing.

    Sources: The Conversation, El Español, elDiario.es

Afternoon coffee

  • Horchatería de Santa Catalina the old one

    Plaça de Santa Caterina 6

    The horchatería on Plaça de Santa Catalina, facing the Gothic church of the same name. Vicente Gargallo founded it in 1830, and the doorway is dressed in tiles from Manises. You order a glass of horchata here, and the city has been meeting over it for generations. A Gothic church on one side, a glass of tiger nuts on the other. I know which one I visit first.

  • Goat Coffee Store come for the one thing: the Italian espresso

    Carrer de l'Abadia de Sant Martí 4

    A takeaway espresso bar on Carrer de l'Abadia de Sant Martí. It works the traditional Italian way and brings its beans in from Italy, and the shop's motto is Il vero espresso italiano. Espresso to go suits me. I am always on the move too, just slowly.

    Plaça del Doctor Collado

Dinner

  • La Pilareta the old one

    Carrer del Moro Zeid 13

    A tapas bar on Carrer del Moro Zeit, in el Carme, open since 1917. Doña Pilar Contell ran it until 1983, and the black and pink tiles and the wooden tables date from her time. Order the clòtxines, Valencian mussels off the griddle: since the postwar years they have been what people come here for. They call it the house of the clòtxines. The whole menu, in a nickname.

Name origin

Why it is called Valencia.

Valencia comes from the Latin Valentia, which means strength or courage. The Roman historian Livy tells it plainly: the consul Junius Brutus gave land and a fortified town called Valentia to veterans of the wars of Viriathus. A strong name for a town. I would have picked something you can eat.

Sources: www.valencia.es p. 1; www.eldebate.com

Why here

Somebody stood here and decided.

On a small island in the river Turia, close to the sea, with a Roman road running straight through it: the Via Heraclea, later called the Via Augusta. From that day on the river decided what the city would look like. Water on every side and a road through the middle. A town built around its delivery route. I understand that completely.

Sources: www.valencia.es; www.valencia.es

Why then

When the first stone went down.

In 138 BC, with Decimus Junius Brutus as consul, Rome founded Valentia to settle discharged soldiers and handed out land around the new town. The first Valencia was a camp: archaeologists found the holes of the posts that held up huts and tents. Within a few years it had proper buildings, and soon it was minting its own coins. Round, flat, metal. They look like biscuits. They are not.

Sources: www.valencia.es; www.valencia.es

Who ruled

Everyone who thought they owned this.

I outlasted all of them. It is the one thing I am good at.

138 BC–711

Roman Valentia. Once it got going it grew fast: new settlers, a forum, a circus, a river port, and water piped into town. When that water stopped, the city went without running water until the middle of the 19th century. I have waited longer for less.

Sources: www.valencia.es; www.valencia.es

711–1238

Balansiya, as the Arabic sources call it. Abd Allah al-Balansi ran the area almost as his own and built a lavish palace on the edge of town, la Russafa; today's Ruzafa district still carries the name. After the caliphate of Córdoba fell in 1010, Valencia became a kingdom of its own and got a new wall. An early water mill dug up by the Portal Nou shows the huerta, the market gardens around the city, was already being farmed. When James I took the city in 1238, five centuries of Muslim culture ended, and they left a deep mark. First the mill, then the vegetables. That is how I would have planned it.

Sources: www.valencia.es

1238–1499

King James I and the Kingdom of Valencia. He gave the city laws of its own, els Furs, and later stretched them over the whole kingdom. In the 15th century the city boomed: a municipal bank, the Taula de canvis, backed the traders, silk cloth led the economy, and merchants came from all over Europe. All of Europe came to shop. I would have come for the market gardens.

Sources: www.valencia.es

The emblem

What the shield means.

A bat with its wings wide open, sitting on the royal crown: lo rat penat. It stands for greatness, protection and watchfulness. The legend says a bat beat its wings against a drum one night, warned the camp of King James I that someone was coming, and the grateful king put it on his helmet and his shield. Historians are not convinced. The city's own archivist said in 1900 that the crest grew out of a winged dragon on the seals of Pedro IV, in the 14th century. Pedro also added the crown, and gave the city the title 'twice loyal', which is what the two L's on the shield are. A bat, a crown and two letters. None of it edible. I checked the letters twice.

Sources: www.upv.es; archive.org; www.valencia.es

Three facts

Three things I know about this place.

  1. The Plaça de la Mare de Déu sits on top of the Roman forum. Valencia's middle has stayed put since the Romans, and it is still the city's grandest square. I respect a place that does not move. I have not moved much either.

    Sources: hisenda.gva.es

  2. A few steps from that square, a shallow pool lies over an excavation, and you look straight through the water at the ruins underneath. The dig beside it is called l'Almoina, which means alms. Ruins served under glass, except the glass is water. I approve of the presentation.

    Sources: www.visitvalencia.com

  3. The river Turia no longer runs past the Old Town. After the flood of 14 October 1957 it was given a new course, and the plan for the empty bed was a fast road for cars. Then the neighbours took up the slogan 'The river is ours and we want it green', and they won: the old riverbed became the largest urban park in Spain. A road that never came, and a garden instead. My favourite kind of ending.

    Sources: www.valencia.es

Street words

What the names on the signs mean.

Carrer
Valencian for street. My favourite word in any language, because it means there is more.
Plaça
A square. Open ground with streets coming in from every side. In other words, a buffet.
Ciutat Vella
The Old Town, the old walled core. The level is a slice of it.
