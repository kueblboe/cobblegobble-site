The Old Town

Every street, drawn.

Every lane inside that line is a course. The circles are fruit.

Landmarks

What I would eat first.

Landmarks are fruit. The bigger the sight, the bigger the bonus.

  • Salzburger Dom 1,500 points

    You will meet this one from three sides before you ever face the front, because the open stone wraps right around it. Come at it through the arches on the south flank rather than across the paving: it lands harder when you have been in the shade first. Then go in and look up. Main course.

  • Haus für Mozart 500 points

    The whole festival quarter is dug into the cliff, and this is the piece of it you can stand right under without a ticket. Walk into the yard at the foot of the wall: sheer rock on one flank, doors on the other, and a stair climbing the face between them. In summer the yard belongs to everybody. In winter it is mine.

  • Mozarts Geburtshaus 500 points

    Yellow, narrow, and impossible to miss in the busiest lane on this bank. Do not just join the queue at the door. Step back across the cobbles and look up: the shop signs hang far out over the street on wrought iron, because these buildings were too thin to spare any width for lettering. Then go in. I will wait out here with the ironwork.

  • Museum der Moderne 500 points

    The plainest box on the skyline, parked along the ridge above the left bank like a loaf of white bread somebody set down and forgot. Two ways up: the quick one, and the footpath that winds you up the hillside in twenty minutes. Take the footpath. The path along the crest runs right past the entrance, and the view from it is free.

  • Schloss Mirabell 500 points

    Do not stop at the railings. The pull here is the garden: stand at the top of the steps and the rock on the far side of the water drops neatly into the gap between the hedges, which somebody arranged on purpose a long time ago. Afterwards go inside and find the staircase. It is crawling with small stone figures, it is the most excessive object on this side of the river, and standing on it costs nothing.

  • Hettwerbastei 250 points

    A ledge halfway up the quiet side of town, and one of the few high places here that costs nothing and has no queue. The climb to it is short and unkind. What you get for it is the entire opposite side laid out flat, roofs first, with the fortress sitting on top of the arrangement like a lid.

  • Kajetanerplatz 250 points

    The far end of the left bank, where the crowd finally thins and the paving gets wider. Most visitors turn round before they get this far, which is exactly why I recommend it. Bring an empty bottle with you. There is a reason, and I am keeping it to myself.

  • Makartplatz 250 points

    A wide open space on the right bank that most walkers cross at speed on their way to somewhere else. Slow down in the middle of it. One side is shut off by a church whose face curves inward instead of bulging out, which is a difficult and expensive way to close a space, and at the bus stop nobody ever looks at it. I do.

  • Mozartsteg 250 points

    So narrow that two people have to be polite about it in the middle, and the middle is the place to stop. From there the whole left-hand skyline lines up in one row, and what is underneath you is the fastest thing in this town. Then keep going. Moving water under a street changes the texture, and I notice.

  • Müllner Kirche 250 points

    The far north tip of this round, and a proper walk out to get there. Take the path by the water rather than the road: the tower keeps appearing and vanishing between the trees the entire way, which is a good trick for something that never moves. At the top you are well above the roofs, and everything you walked lies flat behind you.

  • Sebastianskirche 250 points

    The busiest shopping lane on this side of the water has an unremarkable doorway in it that almost nobody uses. Go through. The noise stops dead on the other side, there are trees, and in the middle sits a small domed thing lined inside with coloured tiles. Two minutes off your route. Take them.

Eating

Actual food.

The town is named after a preservative and grew rich moving it, and almost nothing on the plate here started in the town. It came down the valleys. Cattle out of the Pinzgau, lambs off the Tennengau pastures, potatoes and rye from a basin at a thousand metres, char out of the cold lakes to the east, vegetables carted in from the flat land under the Untersberg. What the city added was money and a court kitchen that was very good at making plain things complicated. Its most famous dish is three browned hills of beaten egg white. A city that got rich on salt and is remembered for sugar.

With meat

  • Pinzgauer Rind

    A mountain breed out of the Hohe Tauern, whose ancestors the register puts in those valleys with the Celts around 800 BC. It was kept for three jobs at once: milk, meat and pulling. The draught oxen even had their own name here, Übertäuerer, the ones that go over the Tauern. I have never been over a pass and I intend to keep it that way.

    Ask whether it is Original Pinzgauer. The old breeding line is a recognised endangered breed, and the menu does not always say which one you are getting.

    Sources: Register der Traditionellen Lebensmittel (BMLUK), Nr. 29 Pinzgauer Rind

  • Tennengauer Berglamm

    Lamb from the Tennengau, the district upriver towards Hallein, no more than six months old and mild with it. Sheep were the main animal on the high farms here until the end of the 1300s, under a system called Schwaigen that the lords of the land set up themselves in the twelfth century. Somebody has been walking animals up that valley for eight hundred years, which I consider a serious body of work.

    Sources: Register der Traditionellen Lebensmittel (BMLUK), Nr. 72 Tennengauer Berglamm

  • Seesaibling

    Char out of the cold lakes east of town, firm and fine grained, with a mineral taste the register bothers to write down. It lives deep, and for centuries that meant the nobility ate it and nobody else did. Ordinary people got at it once fishing line became thin enough to be invisible in water. A fish that was protected by physics rather than by law.

    Ask which lake it came out of. The Wolfgangsee gave up five thousand kilos in 1957 and a hundred and eighty twenty years later, and stocking is what holds the numbers up now.

    Sources: Register der Traditionellen Lebensmittel (BMLUK), Nr. 233 Seesaibling

Vegetarian

  • Salzburger Nockerln

    Eggs, sugar and flour, and mostly it is the egg white. Three peaks baked golden, buried under icing sugar and carried out on a silver platter, shaped to look like the snowy hills standing round the town. Beating egg white stiff only worked its way into kitchens here after 1700, so this is a young dish doing a very good impression of an old one.

    Sources: Register der Traditionellen Lebensmittel (BMLUK), Nr. 171 Salzburger Nockerln

  • Pinzgauer Kaspressknödel

    Dried bread cubes, onion, parsley, egg and a great deal of strong local cheese, pressed flat instead of rolled round and fried in butter. Pressed is what the dialect word means, and that is where the name comes from. In the Pinzgau, Tuesday, Thursday and Sunday were the dumpling days, which is three days a week with the menu already settled.

    A dumpling meal here comes in two halves, one to water and one to land. Ask for it to land and it arrives with green salad instead of broth.

    Sources: Register der Traditionellen Lebensmittel (BMLUK), Nr. 162 Pinzgauer Kaspressknödel

  • Salzkammergut Käse

    Cheese off the lakes and high pastures east of town, and the milk is the whole argument: the register ties the taste to the alpine plants the animals are standing in. It was also money. In 1390 the farms around there paid their ground rent to a nunnery in cheese, up to twelve rounds of one pound every Sunday from April to September. Rent you can eat. I approve of the entire arrangement.

    Sources: Register der Traditionellen Lebensmittel (BMLUK), Nr. 65 Salzkammergut Käse

Vegan

  • Lungauer Eachtling

    Eachtling is what the Lungau calls a potato, grown in a closed alpine basin at around a thousand metres. It arrived late and it was not welcome. The register says the farmers' sons came home from six years of military service used to eating them, and that is what finally ended the argument. Beans had held those fields until then. Eighty hectares are left today, down from three hundred and sixty six in 1959, and nearly three quarters of them are organic.

    Sources: Register der Traditionellen Lebensmittel (BMLUK), Nr. 17 Lungauer Eachtling

  • Lungauer Tauernroggen

    A winter rye bred for the Lungau since 1924, in trial gardens set up in 1933 at seven mountain farms between eleven hundred and thirteen hundred and fifty metres. Rye takes its pollen off the wind, so keeping the variety true means no other rye may stand anywhere near it. Almost all of the flour still goes to the households that grew it. A grain that hardly ever leaves home. I understand it completely.

    Sources: Register der Traditionellen Lebensmittel (BMLUK), Nr. 100 Lungauer Tauernroggen

  • Walser Gemüse

    Vegetables off the flat land west of town, between the houses and the Untersberg. The trade turns up in writing in 1576, in a family handover contract that still sits in the abbey archive. In 1907 the schoolteachers of Wals asked the imperial ministry to move the school free day from Thursday to Saturday, because seventy six farmers had to get produce to market and needed their children to carry it. The ministry said no. Fifty of those seventy six were delivering into this city.

    Sources: Register der Traditionellen Lebensmittel (BMLUK), Nr. 24 Walser Gemüse

  • Lazarte im Café Wernbacher the old one

    Franz-Josef-Straße, in the Andräviertel across the river. There has been a coffeehouse at number 5 since 1936, and the Wernbacher family opened theirs in it in 1952. The curved walnut furniture designed for it in the fifties is still in the room. Since June 2024 Alberto Lazarte, from Peru, has run the house, so potatoes, quinoa and spices from home now sit next to the cake. Seventy years of crumbs in one room. I call that a well seasoned floor.

Name origin

Why it is called Salzburg.

Salt named this place. From the end of the 7th century the bishop here held the biggest share in the salt works at Reichenhall, and a few decades after that the town's German name was simply the name of the cargo. Before that it was Iuvavum, a Celtic word nobody can eat. Salzburg turns up in writing around 755 and has been left alone ever since. A whole city named after a seasoning. I can work with that.

Sources: www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de, austria-forum.org

Why here

Somebody stood here and decided.

Everybody here started high up. The Celts sat on the Kapuzinerberg, the Rainberg, the Bürglstein and the Festungsberg, which makes sense if what you want is to be left alone. Rome arrived in 15 BC, emptied the hilltops and put a town down on the flat by the left bank instead, because a hilltop keeps you alive and a riverbank makes you rich. The valley opens into a basin here at the northern edge of the Alps, with the water cut straight through the middle of it and the hills standing round the rim. That is why this round has two halves and a crossing in between.

Sources: austria-forum.org, www.bda.gv.at

Why then

When the first stone went down.

Twice, and the second time out of the first one's remains. Rome built Iuvavum down on the flat, and Claudius gave it municipal rights around the year 45. Then the Roman administration fell apart, the valley emptied, and the place went quiet for two hundred years. About 696 a bishop called Rupert turned up, the Bavarian duke made him a present of the ruins, and he founded the monastery of Sankt Peter inside them. A city passed on second hand, and it held.

Sources: austria-forum.org

Who ruled

Everyone who thought they owned this.

I outlasted all of them. It is the one thing I am good at.

45–500

Rome. The town on the flat had municipal rights from about the year 45 and sat in the province of Noricum for four centuries. Then the administration came apart. A Christian community and a monastery are recorded here in the late 400s, and part of the old population simply moved back up onto the fortified terrace above the town, which is where the whole thing had started.

Sources: austria-forum.org

739–1803

The bishops, and then the bishops as princes. Salzburg became a bishop's seat in 739 and was raised to an archbishopric in 798. After that the churchmen spent five hundred years turning a diocese into a country: a king confirmed their jurisdiction in 1278, they issued their own body of law in 1328, and by 1342 an archbishop was calling the place his Land in writing. None of that was decided down in the lanes.

Sources: austria-forum.org, www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de

1803–1816

Thirteen years, four owners. The secularisation of 1803 ended the prince-archbishopric and made Salzburg the seat of an electorate. In 1805 it went to the Austrian empire, in 1810 Bavaria took possession, and in 1816 the Treaty of Munich handed it back to Austria. Whole countries changed hands over this valley in less time than it takes a lane to wear down a step.

Sources: www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de

1816–

Austria, at last, and slowly. The title said Duchy of Salzburg from 1816, but for decades it was administered as one district of the province next door, with the courts and the offices sitting in Linz. It became a crown land of its own in 1850, in practice 1861, and the city got its own municipal statute in 1869. Since November 1918 it has been a federal state, and the town I lie in is its capital.

Sources: www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de, austria-forum.org

The emblem

What the shield means.

A silver crenellated wall on red, with the gate standing open, and three towers behind it under gold roofs. The middle tower is broader and taller than the other two, and that is the whole argument. The arms go back to a town seal in use here since 1249, and on that seal the tall middle tower is the tower of the town hall. Not the cathedral. Not the fortress. In a place run by archbishops for a thousand years, the citizens put their own roof on the shield and left the gate wide open. The open gate is the part I approve of.

Sources: www.ris.bka.gv.at, wiki.sn.at

Emblem: Public domain — official coat of arms (Wikimedia Commons)

Three facts

Three things I know about this place.

  1. The big squares around the cathedral are not generosity. They are holes. Archbishop Wolf Dietrich set out to turn a cramped late-Gothic town into a German Rome, and to clear the ground for those squares he had fifty-five burgher houses pulled down. So walking out of the tight lanes onto that open stone is walking out of the citizens' town and into the churchmen's one. Two towns, one Old Town, and I can taste the seam.

    Sources: austria-forum.org, www.bda.gv.at

  2. In 996 the emperor gave this town three rights in one go: to hold a market, to take a toll, and to strike its own coin. That is three separate ways of earning a living off people walking past. I have exactly one, and it is slower than all of them.

    Sources: austria-forum.org

  3. There were two rings of wall here, the first raised between the 11th and 13th centuries and the second between 1465 and 1480, with a further belt of works added in the 1620s and 1630s. Then in 1860 the city stopped counting as a fortress, and in 1866 the emperor made the town a present of the works between Schloss Mirabell and the Linzertor. They came down between 1867 and 1874, and the stone never left: it went into the riverbanks and the drains. A town that ate its own wall is a town I can learn from.

    Sources: www.stadt-salzburg.at, wiki.sn.at

Street words

What the names on the signs mean.

Gasse
A lane, and the ordinary word for a street here. Getreidegasse, Goldgasse, Judengasse, Steingasse, Brodgasse, Pfeifergasse. Count the names ending in -gasse and you have most of your day accounted for.
Gässchen
The same thing, made smaller. Badergässchen, Döllerergässchen, Königsgässchen, Sterngässchen. When a lane here is too narrow to be called a lane, it gets a diminutive instead of more room.
Passage
A way straight through the inside of a building, and this town is riddled with them. Azwanger Passage, Fabrizi Passage, Aula Passage, Rathauspassage, Schmuckpassage, and the Schatz Durchhaus, which says the same thing in plainer German. The block is the wall and the passage is the bite.
Stiege
A stair that counts as a street. Imbergstiege, Nonnbergstiege, Michael-Haydn-Stiege, Alma-Rosé-Stiege. The ground here goes up at both edges, so some of the level is vertical. Steps have dots on them too.
Kai
The embankment along the water, walled and paved once the river was straightened. Elisabethkai, Franz-Josef-Kai, Giselakai, Rudolfskai. One imperial family took all four. Efficient.
Steg
A footbridge, as against a Brücke, which has to carry cars. Müllnersteg, Marko-Feingold-Steg. On this round the Stege are the good crossings and the Staatsbrücke is the loud one.
