Landmarks

Sights in Buxtehude: what I would eat first.

Landmarks are fruit. The bigger the sight, the bigger the bonus.

  • St. Petri-Kirche 1,500 points

    You see its tower long before you find the door. Inside waits an organ from 1859 with 3,300 pipes, which I can only read as a great many straws. Then look all the way up, to the very top, and decide for yourself what is sitting there.

  • Marschtorzwinger 500 points

    A piece of the town's old defences that never left. Its walls are up to 1.90 m thick, which to anyone else is protection and to me is a very long lunch. Count the narrow openings somebody once peered out of.

  • Rathaus Buxtehude 500 points

    Built in 1913 by an architect who had trained at the building school in this very town. Homework, handed in a few years late. Take your time at the entrance: it is telling a joke, and it is a slow one. I like slow.

  • Fleetenkieker 250 points

    Someone stands by the old harbour and has not gone home in years. Same spot, every weather. I know the feeling: stones are good at that too. Spend a minute beside him and find out what he is keeping an eye on.

  • Flethmühle Buxtehude 250 points

    A tall building at the end of the old harbour. For centuries it did one job: turning a river into flour. That is the most sensible use of a river I have ever heard of. What it does now, I am not telling you.

  • Hase-und-Igel-Brunnen 250 points

    The town's most famous race, stopped mid-stride. One runner has long legs. The other has a plan. I have neither, only patience, which is slower than both and has never once lost. Come and see who is ahead.

Eating

What to eat in Buxtehude: actual food.

Buxtehude sits on the edge of the Altes Land, the Elbe marsh between Stade and Hamburg and the largest unbroken fruit-growing area in northern Europe. So the town eats like the marsh: apples, fish from the Elbe, and soups that are allowed to be sweet. I start with the apples.

Meat and fish

Two. Nothing else with meat in it is tied to this marsh by three independent sources. Labskaus belongs to Hamburg and the coast.

  • Stint

    Small Elbe smelt, fried. In the Altes Land it has long been a cult dish, and when the season opens in spring, the restaurants along the Elbe announce it.

    Spring only. Ask whether it is on the board.

    Sources: TAGEBLATT, taz, Nordische Esskultur

  • Altländer Hochzeitssuppe

    The Altes Land wedding soup. The local version is bound with egg and cream, strong, and comes with beef, raisins and raisin bread. Raisins in the soup and raisins in the bread. I call that thorough.

    Sources: TAGEBLATT, Nordische Esskultur, foodhunter

Vegetarian

One. The marsh kitchen runs on cream, eggs and butter, so its meat-free classics are sweet, and the apple in yeast dough is the one a register vouches for. The apple soup on the vegan list suits you too.

  • Apfelknaust

    A whole apple wrapped in yeast dough and baked. In the Altes Land, when dough is left over from the apple cake, this is simply the obvious next step. Other regions call it an apple in a dressing gown. Leftover dough, one whole apple, nothing wasted. That is my kind of housekeeping.

    Sources: Spezialitäten aus Niedersachsen, WDR

Vegan

One dish. The marsh kitchen runs on cream, eggs, butter and Elbe fish, and the apple soup is the one classic that needs none of them.

  • Altländer Apfelsuppe

    Sweet apple soup from the Altes Land: sour apples, cinnamon and raisins, sometimes pumpkin. It tastes very sweet and works warm, or cold in a hot summer.

    Some kitchens bind it with cream or egg yolk. Ask.

    Sources: Spezialitäten aus Niedersachsen, Varta-Führer, dein-niedersachsen.de

Name origin

Why it is called Buxtehude.

The name is read as "the landing place by the beeches". In 959 a farming settlement called Buochstadon turns up in a document, and the town reads that as "place of beeches". By 1135 the place is Buchstadihude: a hude, Middle Low German for a landing place on a river bank, had grown up on the Este. Say Buchstadihude fast a few times and it folds into Buxtehude, which is what happened soon after. I like a name that chewed itself down.

Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Stadtporträt; Hansestadt Buxtehude, Von wegen märchenhaft: Buxtehude – ein ganz realer Ort

Why here

Somebody stood here and decided.

On a seam. Dry Geest on one side, wet marsh on the other, and a strip of moor in between. In 1285 the Archbishop of Bremen founded the new town right in that moor strip, one and a half kilometres from the old settlement on the Geest edge. Two kinds of land next to each other means two kinds of goods to swap, and that made Buxtehude a trading place. Two kinds of ground in one bite. Very efficient.

Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Stadtporträt; Hansestadt Buxtehude, Von wegen märchenhaft: Buxtehude – ein ganz realer Ort

Why then

When the first stone went down.

1285, and on purpose. In Hamburg, Lüneburg and Stade the harbour had crept from one bank of the river to the other. Buxtehude was the first German town built to a plan around a central harbour basin, laid out by Dutch specialists for the Archbishop of Bremen. There was a yearly fair by 1287, Stade town law in 1328, and by 1363 the town was an active member of the Hanse. Most towns grow like moss. This one was drawn first. I appreciate a meal that comes with a menu.

Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Stadtporträt; Hansestadt Buxtehude, Denkmalpflege; Hansestadt Buxtehude, Von wegen märchenhaft: Buxtehude – ein ganz realer Ort

Who ruled

Everyone who thought they owned this.

I outlasted all of them. It is the one thing I am good at.

1285–1648

An Archbishop of Bremen founded the town, and the archbishops stayed its lords. Under them Buxtehude got the Stade town law in 1328 and turned up as an active member of the Hanse in 1363, rich on grain shipped out of the Elbe marshes and a growing craft trade. It even sent envoys to the Hanseatic diets. Grain. Everything comes back to grain with me.

Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Von wegen märchenhaft: Buxtehude – ein ganz realer Ort; Hansestadt Buxtehude, Stadtporträt

1648–1712

In 1648 Buxtehude came under Swedish rule, and the convents were secularised. Late in the 17th century, Buxtehude shipowners ran sea-going ships on a trading loop from Hamburg to Archangelsk, on to Italy and back to Hamburg. A small harbour with a very long reach. I have never been further than the next street, and I am fine with that.

Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Von wegen märchenhaft: Buxtehude – ein ganz realer Ort; Hansestadt Buxtehude, Stadtporträt

1715–1866

After a short Danish occupation from 1712 to 1715, Buxtehude became Hanoverian: first in the Electorate of Hanover, from 1814 in the Kingdom. In 1837 the high road from Harburg to Stade was laid straight through town. In 1866 the town became Prussian, and in 1881 the railway arrived. A road, then rails. Asphalt was on its way, and nobody asked me.

Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Von wegen märchenhaft: Buxtehude – ein ganz realer Ort

The emblem

What the shield means.

Two gold keys crossed on blue, pointing up, with a black cross above the point where they meet. The picture goes back to the town's oldest seal, already hanging on a document of 1304. The keys are St Peter's keys, and they also point to the town's lord, the Archbishop of Bremen. The black cross came from the Diocese of Verden, which the town's church belonged to in the Middle Ages. Two keys and not a single door. I have checked.

Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Von wegen märchenhaft: Buxtehude – ein ganz realer Ort

Emblem: Public domain — official coat of arms (Wikimedia Commons)

Three facts

Three things I know about this place.

  1. The harbour is the plan. The Fleth, the basin dug into the middle of town, counts alongside St Petri as Buxtehude's most important monument, because it still shows how the town was laid out: the Viver, the old moat, flows round the outside, and the harbour was cut into the inside. A town with its dinner plate in the centre. Sensible.

    Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Fleth, Flethmühle und Ewer Margareta

  2. Walk the Westfleth and the houses change their clothes as you go. The 16th century built in half-timber, with carvings, inscriptions and upper floors that lean out over the street. Westfleth 23 and 25 are two baroque houses, flat-fronted, whose only decoration is a curved doorway. From the 18th century on, white plaster takes over along the Fleth. Timber to plaster. I have watched fashions come and go, mostly slowly.

    Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Häuser am Westfleth als Ensemble

  3. The old moat is still there, and it still goes all the way round. You can follow the Viver once around the town centre. A ring of water with a town in the middle. To me it looks like a soup bowl.

    Sources: Hansestadt Buxtehude, Kulturpfad Hanse, Wasser, Hafen

Street words

What the names on the signs mean.

Fleth
The harbour basin dug right into the middle of town. The street along its west side is the Westfleth.
Viver
The town's old moat. It still flows round the centre, and a path follows it.
