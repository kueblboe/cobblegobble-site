Landmarks

What I would eat first.

Landmarks are fruit. The bigger the sight, the bigger the bonus.

  • York Minster 1,500 points

    The one you can see from everywhere, and the reason half the lanes here bend the way they do. Do the outside first, all the way round, close to the stone. On the north side the ground falls away and the thing grows on you. I have never managed the whole lap in one sitting.

  • Clifford's Tower 500 points

    It sits on its own small rise in the south-east of the level, on the far side of the car park, and there are steps. Take them. From the top the town lies out flat and you can finally see how the two rivers pinch it. Best view I get from anywhere that is not a wall.

  • Jorvik Viking Centre 500 points

    It is downstairs, beneath a shopping street, which is exactly where I would want it. You wait your turn up at street level and then go below the surface you have been walking on all day. Down is where a street keeps the parts it does not show you.

  • St Mary's Abbey 500 points

    North-west of the level, inside the Museum Gardens, where the lanes finally stop and there is grass. Late in the day is the hour for it, when the arches throw their shadows a long way across the lawn. Sit down on the grass a while. I have, for about eight hundred years.

  • Kings Square 250 points

    A small open space right in the middle of everything, with the Shambles running off one corner of it. Everybody halts here to work out which way they are going, and everybody is looking at head height. This is one of the few spots in town where the interesting part is below your boots.

  • Micklegate Bar 250 points

    The far west end of the level, up at the top of Micklegate. Stop underneath before you walk through and look at the ceiling of the arch. Then cross the road and look back. From over there it is a different building, and I have never decided which of the two I like.

  • Monk Bar 250 points

    North-east corner, and the biggest of the four. If you are doing the wall walk from the Minster this is where it sets you back down on the street, so most visitors go under it twice without once looking up. Look up. More carving on it than on anything in York that is not a church.

  • Walmgate Bar 250 points

    The far east end, and the longest walk out to it. That is the point of it. The wall runs a long straight line towards it and the town thins out behind you as you go, until it is just you, the rampart and a great deal of sky. Turn round at the top of the steps and look back at what you ate.

  • York Castle Museum 250 points

    South end of the level, down by the water, two heavy blocks with a lawn between them. From outside it gives away nothing at all, which is the best trick in this town. Set aside an afternoon for it. I mean a real one.

Name origin

Why it is called York.

The Romans wrote it Eboracum, a Latin coat thrown over a Celtic name that most likely meant the place of a man called Eburos. Eburos did not keep it long. The word sounded like Boar Town to Roman ears, and an altar found at Bordeaux gives the city a boar as a punning badge. Modern York is what is left of Eboracum after eighteen centuries of mouths. A man turned into a pig, and the pig wore down to four letters.

Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk

Why here

Somebody stood here and decided.

The Vale of York is flat, wet ground that still floods, and at the time this all began it may well have been waterlogged. Two ridges of boulder clay and gravel cross the valley, dumped there by ice, and they were the only dry way over. York sits on the northern one, on a low plateau between the Ouse and the Foss, and the Ouse is tidal this far up, so boats could reach it. A dry line across a wet floor, with water transport at the end of it. Put anything there and the whole country has to walk past your door.

Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk

Why then

When the first stone went down.

In the year 71 Petillius Cerialis took the Ninth Legion out of Lincoln to campaign in the north, and left it standing here. That is the whole founding: five thousand men needed somewhere dry to put their feet. The first fortress was an earth bank and a timber palisade. Stone arrived early in the second century, and on two sides of the city the medieval wall is still sitting on top of it.

Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk, www.historyofyork.org.uk, www.british-history.ac.uk

Who ruled

Everyone who thought they owned this.

I outlasted all of them. It is the one thing I am good at.

71–400

Rome, and for most of that it was a garrison rather than a town. The Ninth Legion put the fortress up in 71 and the Sixth took over from them not long after, for reasons nobody has ever worked out. When Severus divided Britain into two provinces, York became the capital of the lower one. Later an emperor died here, and the soldiers named his son in his place. That is the most consequential thing that has ever happened in this city, and it took an afternoon.

Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk, www.historyofyork.org.uk

627–866

The kings of Northumbria. On Easter Sunday, 12 April 627, King Edwin was christened here in a church of wood he had put up in a hurry while he was still being taught what he was agreeing to. Northumbria was one of the largest kingdoms in Britain and it ran York for two and a half centuries. Almost none of that is above ground now.

Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk

866–954

The Scandinavian kings. The Danish army came out of East Anglia in 866 and took the city on 1 November, and for most of the next ninety years York was the seat of a Norse kingdom with its own rulers. The last of them was driven out in 954 and that was the end of it. What they left behind is not a building. It is the street names, and you will be reading those all afternoon.

Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk

1154–1396

The king, held at arm's length, and the citizens buying their way out from under his sheriff one privilege at a time. York's first royal charter, dated somewhere between 1154 and 1158, confirmed the citizens' liberties, laws and customs and their Merchant Guild. By 1213 a royal writ was addressed to the mayor of York, so by then there was one. Every single one of those rights was paid for in cash. The method here for two hundred and forty years was to make yourself expensive and then wait.

Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk

1396–

The city itself. In 1396 Richard II gave York the status of a county in its own right. The mayor became escheator, and two sheriffs took over from the three bailiffs and held their own county court once a month. With that charter, says the county history, the medieval liberties of York were full-grown. It has had a mayor and it has had sheriffs ever since, which is the same arrangement running for six hundred and thirty years. I like a town that settles on something.

Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk

The emblem

What the shield means.

The city's own shield is a silver field with a red cross on it and five small gold lions walking along the cross. Handsome on a banner. At the size of an icon it is a red plus sign with crumbs on it. So the glyph is the Minster's west end instead: two tall towers at the corners, a squarer one behind and above them over the crossing, and one huge window in the middle. That is the shape this town shows you from the end of every long street, which is exactly what an icon is for.

Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk, historicengland.org.uk

Three facts

Three things I know about this place.

  1. The walls, the ditches, the castle, the fishpond of the Foss and the two rivers together made a ring that went right round the city, and the restored wall still follows the whole of that circuit but for three short gaps. It is a footpath now. In 1829 the York Footpath Association drew up the first proper plan to put it back in order, section by section, and since the 1880s the corporation has kept the entire round open as a public walk. A defence that gave up and became a path. I approve of the career change.

    Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk

  2. Two of the streets on this level are Roman roads wearing Norse names. The legionary fortress covered just under fifty acres, and its two main roads crossed at right angles in the middle of it. One of them is Stonegate. The other is Petergate, High and Low. Everything above has been knocked down and rebuilt several times over, and the lines underneath have not moved an inch. A street is much harder to get rid of than a building.

    Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk

  3. For about a century this was a sweet-making town. In 1851 the second largest employer in York was Joseph Terry, confectioner, of St Helen's Square, which you cross on this level. By 1901 the census counted 1,194 chocolate and cocoa makers here, and another 800 people making jams, preserves and sweets. Rowntree alone went from 100 workers in 1879 to 4,066 thirty years later. I eat streets, and even I think that is a lot of sugar.

    Sources: www.british-history.ac.uk

Street words

What the names on the signs mean.

gate
Not a gate. From Old Norse gata, a way or a street, and this town is full of them: Stonegate, Coppergate, Goodramgate, Fossgate, Swinegate, Colliergate, Micklegate. Micklegate is simply the big one.
bar
The gates in the wall, on the other hand, are bars. So the streets are gates and the gates are bars. Bar Lane and Monk Bar Court are named after them, and nobody here finds this confusing, which I admire.
yard
A slot between two buildings that opens into a courtyard behind. Coffee Yard, Nelson's Yard, Hurst's Yard, Melroses Yard, Dixons Yard. Some of them are barely shoulder-width, and those are my favourites.
staith
A landing place on the river, where boats tied up and unloaded. King's Staith on one bank, Queen Staith Road and Queen's Staith Mews on the other. Both banks go under when the Ouse comes up, and the Ouse comes up.
garth
A piece of ground with a fence round it, from Old Norse gardr, the same word behind garden and yard. Bartle Garth and Pond Garth are the two that are left.
court
A named dead end off a bigger street, usually carrying the name of whoever owned it. Precentor's Court, Cordwainers Court, Lady Anne Court, Bishops Court, Hothams Court. I have firm views about dead ends and I am keeping them to myself here, because these ones are pretty.
