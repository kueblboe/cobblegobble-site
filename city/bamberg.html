The Old Town

Every street, drawn.

Every lane inside that line is a course. The circles are fruit.

Landmarks

What I would eat first.

Landmarks are fruit. The bigger the sight, the bigger the bonus.

  • Bamberger Dom 1,500 points

    Four towers, and a choir at either end, so the Dom has two faces and no back at all. Walk all the way around it before you go inside. Then find the man on the horse in there and work out who he is. Nobody has settled that one yet.

  • Alte Hofhaltung 500 points

    The broad wall on the square gives away nothing at all. Go in under the arch anyway. On the far side is a yard, closed in on every side by long half-timbered wings with open wooden galleries along them, and the noise stops at the gate. Most of the crowd out on the square never finds out.

  • Altes Rathaus 500 points

    Everyone takes the same photograph from the same bridge. Take it, then go to the upstream side and lean over. A wooden house is sitting out there on the cutwater, in the current, with a whole building's worth of nerve.

  • E.T.A.-Hoffmann-Theater 500 points

    A small square down at the southern edge, and the narrow house across from the playhouse is the point of coming. The man whose initials are on the sign kept house up there, and it is a museum now: two floors, low ceilings, steep stairs.

  • Schlenkerla 500 points

    Barely wide enough for two, and the busiest strip of pavement on the hill. Everyone stands about in the lane here, because that is what happens outside this door, in any weather, and it has been happening for a very long time. Come down off the slope and see how tight it gets.

  • Ausblick auf Klein Venedig 250 points

    This is the picture on every postcard of the town, and it is still worth the two minutes. Come late in the afternoon, when the light is on the far bank. Flat, short, and the only thing you have to do when you arrive is look.

  • Gabelmann 250 points

    The meeting point. Everybody in this town gives directions from this corner, so you will hear the name said out loud before you get there. It sits where the busy streets cross on the flat side, and it is the easiest thing to find on the whole level.

  • Marienbrunnen 250 points

    The far eastern corner of the map, and the quietest thing on it. Nobody walks out this far for it, which is exactly why I do. Houses on every side of a plain square, one thing in it worth stopping at, and nothing else asking for anything.

  • Sankt Gangolf 250 points

    Out east, over the far arm of the river and past the line where the visitors turn back. That is the whole reason to go. The lanes on that side are wider, lower and greener than anything up on the hill, and the way out to it is flat the entire distance.

  • Villa Concordia 250 points

    The side you meet first is not the side that matters. The good one faces south, down two wings set square to each other, over a walled terrace garden with a cascade running down it to the river. So cross at the next bridge and look back from the far bank.

Eating

Actual food.

People from Bamberg get called onion treaders. When the onion tops shot up too far, the gardeners strapped little boards to their shoes and walked the fields flat, so the strength went down into the bulb. The same gardeners dug licorice and sold it into Bohemia, Austria and Hungary. This town eats out of the ground first and out of the kitchen second, and the list below keeps that order.

With meat

  • Blaue Zipfel

    Raw Franconian bratwurst, simmered in a broth of vinegar, onions and spice until the skins turn faintly blue. That colour is the entire name. In Franconia you eat them straight out of the broth, with a slice of wood oven bread beside them. I like a dish that is named after what it looks like.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern, Blaue Zipfel

  • Kesselfleisch

    Pork simmered slowly in a good broth, with salt and pepper and nothing else. Around Bamberg the cut is belly, and a fair share of fat is the point rather than the problem. It comes with bread, or with sauerkraut and dumplings. If a sausage bursts in the kettle, the broth becomes soup, and here that counts as a result.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern, Kesselfleisch

  • Fränkischer Sauerbraten

    A roast braised on the stove in its own marinade, which in Franconia means Sunday. The sauce turns sweet and sour because Soßenlebkuchen, a gingerbread with sauce in its name, gets stirred into it. Red cabbage and raw potato dumplings come alongside. A gingerbread that ends up in the gravy has found its calling.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern, Fränkischer Sauerbraten

Vegetarian

  • Bamberger Hörnchen

    Puff pastry rolled into a crescent, crustier and slimmer than a croissant and at least twenty per cent butter. You dunk it in your coffee before you bite. Bamberg's town book names a crescent baker behind St. Martin in 1483, about two hundred years before Vienna claims the shape. I am not taking sides. I am only doing the subtraction.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern, Bamberger

  • Bamberger Hörnla

    A small potato, finger shaped and slightly bent, with a nutty taste and a firm bite. Bamberg's own kitchen saves it for the festive potato salad, and otherwise it arrives tossed in butter. It shares its name with the pastry above, and the register warns you about that itself. All of Franconia grows about fifteen hectares of it.

    Ask whether they are really Hörnla. The register says they are more and more often swapped for the higher yielding La Ratte.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern, Bamberger Hörnla

  • Bamberger Wirsinggemüse

    Pointed savoy cabbage, more tender than the round kind. The Bamberg version is a purée: the cooked leaves go through the coarse plate of a mincer so you can still feel them, then get bound with a butter roux and softened onion. The register calls it indispensable next to roast goose. It is also perfectly good next to nothing.

    Its usual place is beside roast goose, duck or pork. Ask for it as a dish of its own.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern, Bamberger Spitzwirsing

Vegan

Two, and both are things a gardener grew rather than things a kitchen cooked. Market gardening here is documented from the fourteenth century, and the gardeners still sell at the Grüner Markt and from their own farm shops. Bamberg's plant food mostly left the garden raw. The moment a cook got hold of it, butter and roux went in.

  • Bamberger Rettich

    A slim white radish about thirty centimetres long, soft as butter and properly sharp. Slice it thin, salt it and leave it a few minutes. In Bamberg they say the radish has to weep. It is an old local variety, and only a few of the town's gardeners still have it.

    A Rettichbrot is radish on buttered bread. Ask for the radish on its own.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern, Bamberger Rettich

  • Bamberger Süßholz

    Licorice root. Bamberg is thought to be the oldest place in Germany that grew it, and a treatise from 1536 already mentions it here. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the gardeners dug two to three hundred hundredweight a year and sold a hundred and fifty of them into Bohemia, Austria and Hungary. Children here chewed a piece for the sweet juice. I chew streets, so I understand.

    Chew it for the juice and leave the wood.

    Sources: GenussErbe Bayern, Süßholz

Late breakfast

  • Café Müller the old one

    A café since 1910, kept in the manner of a Viennese coffee house. Breakfast is the reason to come, at seven to ten euros, and an Ottoman breakfast sits on the card beside the usual one. Crêpes, sandwiches and cake cover the hours after that. A hundred and sixteen years of breakfasts is a great many crumbs, and I mean that as praise.

Afternoon coffee

  • Café Riffelmacher the old one

    A confectioner's café founded in 1831, right below the Altes Rathaus on the Obere Brücke. Tortes, pralines and ice cream are made in house. Order the Engadine nut cake, which sits on a crisp nut shortcrust base. Nearly two hundred years at the same bridge. I know the feeling, and nobody ever brought me cake.

    The hours change with the season. In winter it closes at six.

    Sources: inFranken.de (Fränkischer Tag), Sandro Frank, Top-Cafés in Bamberg: Das sind die Lieblinge unserer Leser, 11.07.2026, Bamberg lieben (Saskia Schwarzenberger), Riffelmacher, 11.01.2019, City Blog Bamberg (Robert Krick Verlag), Eiskalt verliebt: Die schönsten Eisdielen in Bamberg, 18.07.2025

  • Seven Hills Coffee Roasters worth the detour

    A thirty square metre coffee store in the Habergasse, run by two barista champions who roast their own beans. Espresso and filter in several brewing methods, with changing coffees, plus chai and lemonade. You drink it out in the lane, inside, or in a small covered courtyard. Thirty square metres is about the size of one good cake, and I have no complaints.

Name origin

Why it is called Bamberg.

The hill was named first and the rest of the town borrowed it. Babenberg is a personal name, probably a Babo, stuck onto the Old High German word for a hill. Then the scribes went to work on it: Babenberh in 902, Papinberc in 973, Bavanberg around 1001, and Bamberg only from 1174. Nobody here named anything after the water.

Sources: www.koeblergerhard.de, www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de

Why here

Somebody stood here and decided.

Two halves, picked for two different reasons. The high side is the last of the Steigerwald running out towards the river, cut into steep spurs with deep valleys between them, which is exactly what you want underneath a castle. There has been a castle up there since at least the ninth century, as far as anyone can tell. The low side is an island, held between the two main arms of the Regnitz. One half is defensible and the other half is useful, and this town took both.

Sources: www.geodaten.bayern.de p. 1, www.geodaten.bayern.de p. 2

Why then

When the first stone went down.

There are two dates and only one of them decided anything. The castle on the hill is written down in 902. It was a good enough address that a Bavarian duke rebuilt it for himself, as a seat rather than a stronghold. That duke became king, had no children to leave it all to, and wanted his hill to be a bishopric instead. The negotiations were long and difficult, because a new diocese had to be cut out of two neighbours' ground, and Würzburg and Eichstätt had to agree to lose it. They agreed on 1 November 1007. Everything on this walk grew out of that argument.

Sources: www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de

Who ruled

Everyone who thought they owned this.

I outlasted all of them. It is the one thing I am good at.

902–1007

The Babenbergs, and then the crown. The castle on the hill was theirs. Early in the tenth century they lost a long quarrel with a rival house, and what they held around Bamberg became imperial property. By the end of that century the hill was the private seat of a Bavarian duke, who rebuilt it as somewhere to live well rather than somewhere to hold out.

Sources: www.deutsche-biographie.de, www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de

1007–1802

The bishops, and they were princes as well. The church here became a bishopric in 1007, but a bishop was only an imperial prince once the emperor had invested him, and that investiture is what brought the coinage, the tolls, the mines, the right of safe conduct and the courts with it. By the end of the eighteenth century the territory held between a hundred and fifty and a hundred and ninety-five thousand people, two further capitals at Forchheim and Kronach, fifteen more towns and fifteen markets. It was never a closed country. Other people's lordships sat inside it like currants in a bun.

Sources: www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de

1802–

Bavaria. On 22 November 1802 the elector of Bavaria issued the patent taking possession of the Franconian territories awarded to him, and a week after that, on 29 November, the last prince-bishop gave up the government. The document that made it lawful, the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss, only turned up on 25 February 1803. Eight hundred years of arrangement, wound up in a week and legalised afterwards.

Sources: www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de

The emblem

What the shield means.

Bamberg put a person on its shield, not an animal. He stands on a red field in silver armour with a red cross on his chest, a small silver pennant with a red cross on it in his right hand, and a blue shield carrying a silver eagle propped up under his left. The icon keeps the figure and the pennant staff, because at this size that is all that survives. There is one of him in stone here too: Joseph Bonaventura Mutschele cut a city coat of arms in 1755 and 1756 for the town hall, and it stands today in the arcades at Geyerswörth. It has not moved in two hundred and seventy years. I know the feeling.

Sources: de.wikipedia.org, www.geodaten.bayern.de p. 44

Emblem: Public domain — official coat of arms (Wikimedia Commons)

Three facts

Three things I know about this place.

  1. The bishopric was founded in 1007 and prepared from 1004 at the latest, and it was an imperial move at least as much as a church one, so the notion of founding a second Rome here was never far off. The churches were then laid out across the whole place as a cross. The cathedral chapter, St. Michael and St. Stephan fixed one axis, and two more collegiate churches closed the figure in 1058 and 1071. Only after that could the scattered settlements down here be seen together as one town at all. That is a great deal of plan for somewhere that looks improvised.

    Sources: www.geodaten.bayern.de p. 1

  2. The flat half of this level is five streets, and all five end up in the same place. Grüner Markt, Lange Straße, Kapuzinerstraße, Austraße and Keßlerstraße run together like the fingers of a hand towards the two bridges in the middle. Two of the five are markets, and which of those two came first is still an open question; by the twelfth century they were both there. Whichever finger you start on, you come out at the water.

    Sources: www.geodaten.bayern.de p. 2

  3. East of the right arm of the river the town stops behaving like a town. That side is the Gärtnerstadt, the gardeners' quarter, and it still looks half rural: low houses with their gables to the street and large open growing ground behind them. The gardeners' settlements attached themselves to the Theuerstadt and to the old Steinweg from the fourteenth century at the latest, split into an upper and a lower one. You walk both of those names on this level. Theuerstadt is still called that, and part of the Steinweg is now Obere Königstraße. A place that has been growing its own dinner for seven hundred years has my full attention.

    Sources: www.geodaten.bayern.de p. 3

Street words

What the names on the signs mean.

Gasse
A lane, and there is no shortage: Habergasse, Hasengasse, Lebergasse, Rosengasse, Zwerggasse, Plattnergasse, Mautgasse. Anything still too narrow for that gets called a Gäßchen, like the Balthasargäßchen.
Brücke
A bridge. Obere Brücke, Untere Brücke, Nonnenbrücke, Marienbrücke, Geyerswörthbrücke, Bischofsmühlbrücke, Obere and Untere Mühlbrücke. The river runs down both sides of the town here, so nothing on this level is crossed only once.
wörth
An island, or a piece of dry ground with water around it. Zinkenwörth, Mühlwörth, and the whole Geyerswörth family: a platz, a straße and a brücke. Where a name ends in wörth, the water used to be nearer than it looks now.
berg
A hill, and in this town that means a climb. Katzenberg, Unterer Kaulberg, Oberer Stephansberg, Unterer Stephansberg. Half of this level is flat and half of it is not, and the names tell you which half you are standing on.
Obere / Untere
Upper and lower, used here constantly: Obere and Untere Brücke, Obere and Untere Sandstraße, Obere and Untere Mühlbrücke, Oberer and Unterer Stephansberg. Finding one of a pair is a promise that the other one exists. Two helpings.
Markt
A market square, named after what stood on it. Grüner Markt for greens, Obstmarkt for fruit, Heumarkt for hay. Two of those three are worth crossing a town for and I would rather not discuss the hay.
