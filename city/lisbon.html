Every street on the menu.

Every street in the Old Town of Lisbon is a line of dots you collect by walking it. I have been lying in one of them for six hundred years and I am still not finished.

The Old Town

Every street, drawn.

Every lane inside that line is a course. The circles are fruit.

Landmarks

What I would eat first.

Landmarks are fruit. The bigger the sight, the bigger the bonus.

  • Castelo de São Jorge 1,500 points

    The high point of the level, and every lane up here tips toward it sooner or later. The way up is honest work with no shortcut in it. From the wall you get the river, the roofs and most of this walk in a single look. Worth the legs.

  • Elevador de Santa Justa 500 points

    There is a queue at the bottom and a footbridge at the top. Go up through Chiado, come at it from behind, and you reach the high end with nobody in front of you. A shortcut that is also a longer walk, which is my favourite kind.

  • Igreja de São Domingos 500 points

    The door opens straight off the busiest square here, and hardly anyone turns into it. Step through and the noise stops dead, which is the quickest change of weather you get all day. Then stand still and let your eyes catch up.

  • Praça do Comércio 500 points

    Three sides of stone and one side left open to the water, which is the correct way round. Go to the far end, out between the columns, and take the steps down to where the ground stops being ground. Flattest mouthful on the level.

  • Praça Dom Pedro IV 500 points

    Everybody calls this one by an older name than the one it has, which I find entirely reasonable. Sit at the top end by the theatre, watch the whole city cut across on the diagonal, and count how many take the straight line. Not many ever do.

  • Sé de Lisboa 500 points

    It sits where every lane off the river runs out of climb, so you arrive at it whether you meant to or not. Stand across from it and wait. Sooner or later a yellow tram grinds past the front of it and the picture assembles itself.

  • Chafariz d'El Rei 250 points

    It is set into the hillside on the low road, and most people walk straight past because it reads as a retaining wall with a bit of decoration on it. Stop. Put a hand on it, then look up at how much hill is stacked over your head.

  • Igreja da Conceição Velha 250 points

    It hides on a working street, shoulder to shoulder with the shops, and its face is the oldest thing on the block by a wide margin. Look up before you go in. Then look up again, because the first time you will miss most of it.

  • Miradouro de Santa Luzia 250 points

    You do not climb for this one. It opens off the pavement, which makes it the cheapest reward on the level. Take the far corner under the pergola, put your back against the rail and give it ten minutes. Everybody else gives it forty seconds.

  • Mouraria - Berço do Fado 250 points

    The steepest corner of this walk, and the one still busy being somebody's home. Washing overhead, doorways open, a cat on every third step. Come up slowly. What you came for here is not a building.

  • Museu do Fado 250 points

    The lanes above it are full of doors that open only at night and only with a table booked. This one opens in the afternoon and asks nothing of you. Sit down with the recordings for half an hour and the whole quarter starts to make sense.

  • Museu Militar de Lisboa 250 points

    The far eastern end of the level, down on the flat where the river runs alongside. Most people turn back before they get this far, which I think is a mistake. The long face of it is one of the best walls in this city, and standing in front of it costs nothing.

  • Teatro Nacional de São Carlos 250 points

    A pale face on a paved pocket of a square with no view of anything, which is why nobody waits in it. Come after dark, when the lamps are lit and the doors are open, and the pocket fills up in about four minutes.

Eating

Actual food.

Everything expensive came through this port and kept going. What the city kept was the cheap half. Salt cod off the far north, the parts the butcher could not sell, snails out of a field, chestnuts in a paper cone. The one luxury Lisbon held on to was sugar, and it spent nearly all of it on egg yolks.

With meat

  • Bacalhau

    Cod, split down the belly, salted, matured, then dried. The registered Portuguese cure spends a month under pressure, then another month, before the drying even begins. I am six hundred years old and I still call that patient.

    This is a whole repertoire, not one dish. Look for the day's version on the board rather than on the menu.

    Sources: Produtos Tradicionais Portugueses (DGADR), Bacalhau de Cura Tradicional Portuguesa ETG

  • Iscas com elas

    Isca means the bait on a hook. It also means a thin strip of liver, fried. The elas are the boiled potatoes beside it, and nobody has ever explained why the potatoes get a pronoun.

    Sources: Dicionário Priberam da Língua Portuguesa, isca, Virgílio Nogueiro Gomes, Crónicas: Iscas, Time Out Lisboa, As tascas em Lisboa a não perder

  • Caracóis

    They arrive in May and leave with the heat. A sheet of white paper goes up in the door saying há caracóis, and that is the entire announcement system. Small ones, in the shell, eaten with a toothpick and no cutlery.

    Sources: Time Out Lisboa, Os 10 melhores sítios para comer caracóis em Lisboa, getLISBON, Snails, a Delicacy Loved by the People of Lisbon, NiT, A Casa dos Caracóis já reabriu com os famosos baldes de cinco litros

  • Sardinha assada

    In June the whole city cooks the same fish on the pavement. Charcoal, coarse salt, nothing else. Alfama and Mouraria take it hardest, and this walk goes through both. Six centuries of that smell and I am not tired of it.

    Sources: Renascença, Santo António não é só sardinha ou manjericos, getLISBON, What are the Origins of the Festas de Lisboa?, Time Out Lisboa, Santos Populares em Lisboa: onde jantar antes do arraial

Vegetarian

Two entries, and the reason is history. Old Lisbon cooking is built on salt cod, pork and offal, and the meatless half arrived mostly as pastry. Everyone will tell you the nuns had spare yolks from starching their habits. A Coimbra thesis went looking for that proof and found a story instead. The sweets are real. The reason is not.

  • Pastel de nata

    Puff pastry, egg cream, seven centimetres across, baked at four hundred degrees. The state register says every pastel de nata in the country got its recipe from one 1837 shop. It also says the real version is still a secret. Nearly two centuries of industrial espionage, unsuccessful.

    Cinnamon and icing sugar go on at the counter, not in the oven. Ask for both.

    Sources: Produtos Tradicionais Portugueses (DGADR), Pastéis de Belém

  • Peixinhos da horta

    Little fish from the garden, which are green beans in batter and contain no fish whatsoever. Portuguese missionaries fried them on the church fasting days, the Têmporas, and left the habit behind in Japan. Tempura is this dish, emigrated and never once written home.

    The batter is egg and flour. Ask at the counter if that matters to you.

    Sources: Dicionário Priberam da Língua Portuguesa, peixinho, Virgílio Nogueiro Gomes, Crónicas: Tempura, Time Out Lisboa, Os melhores restaurantes de petiscos em Lisboa

Vegan

Two entries, and both are bought standing up. Lisbon cooks in olive oil rather than butter, so much of the vegetable food is already plant food. The stock underneath it usually is not. Since 2017 Portuguese law has required a meatless option in every public canteen. The modern city is far easier than the old recipes are.

  • Carcaça

    The small white roll. Flour, water, salt, yeast, and a crust that shatters. Everywhere else in the world the small loaf is the expensive one. Here the state register calls it the bread of the people, which is the correct order of things.

    Sources: Produtos Tradicionais Portugueses (DGADR), Pãozinho, Dicionário Priberam da Língua Portuguesa, carcaça

  • Castanhas assadas

    Autumn only. Scored so they do not burst, roasted over charcoal, salted, then wrapped in a cloth to go soft. Sold by the dozen in a paper cone. The register notes that the cone used to be old newspaper, or a page of the phone book.

    Sources: Produtos Tradicionais Portugueses (DGADR), Castanhas Assadas

Late breakfast

  • Nova Pombalina the old one

    Rua do Comércio, at the quiet end of the Baixa. Somebody has been feeding people through this door since 1938, and the Maurício brothers took the place on in 1980. It began as a juice bar and the juices never left. The sandwich to have is the leitão, carved off the roast while you wait. Soup of the day on a chalkboard, a dozen wooden tables, and change from a ten euro note.

    Closed on Sunday, and the shutters come down in the late afternoon. A snack bar, not a dinner plan.

  • As Bifanas do Afonso worth the detour

    Probably the smallest counter in the city, on the Rua da Madalena, and getting on for fifty years old. Thin slices of pork go into the pan at the window and come back out in a bread roll for about three euros fifty. Four people inside is already too many, so nobody sits down. You take it to the bench in the little largo opposite, or you carry it up the hill. A meal that walks with you, which I approve of on principle.

  • Zé dos Cornos where locals go

    Off the Rua da Madalena and down the Beco dos Surradores, at long shared tables on wooden benches. João's grandfather sold coal, firewood and sawdust out of this room. It started feeding people instead, and João has been behind that counter for fifty years. The name is on the wall: his father hung a pair of ox horns up there and the regulars renamed the house around them. Half a portion of ribs, eight euros fifty.

Afternoon coffee

  • Confeitaria Nacional the old one

    Open on the Praça da Figueira since 1829, and still the founder's family running it, generation after generation. His son came back from Paris in 1870 with a cake, and the country has eaten that cake every January since. When the Republic arrived in 1910 somebody proposed renaming it the bolo presidente. Nobody went along with it. A pastry here costs under two euros and the bakery is in the house.

    A counter on the ground floor, and a room with tables up the fan shaped staircase. Same kitchen, different pace.

  • Merendinha do Arco Bandeira where locals go

    Under the Arco Bandeira on the Rua dos Sapateiros, an aluminium counter and three pictures on the wall. Two Galicians opened it in 1944, facing the old Rossio picture house, back when carts still came through the middle of town. The picture that faces you on the way in carries the motto of the house: hygiene, honesty and speed. I have known streets that could not manage two of those. Pataniscas at the counter, standing up, in the middle of the afternoon.

Dinner

  • Estrela da Sé worth the detour

    Six small rooms, each lined in pine, each with a door you can shut. Private cabinets like these were once ordinary in Lisbon and this is one of the last two houses that kept them. Lioz stone table tops, mahogany chairs, and the same cook in that kitchen for fifty two years. The iscas are on the menu, so you can meet that strip of liver in a room of your own.

    There is a break between the midday and the evening service, and the rooms with doors go first. Ask for one on the way in.

    Sources: Lojas com História (Câmara Municipal de Lisboa), Restaurante Estrela da Sé, Círculo das Lojas de Carácter e Tradição de Lisboa, Estrela da Sé (snapshot 2026-05-08, the live page returns a server error), Time Out Lisboa, Estrela da Sé, Restos de Colecção, Restaurante «Estrela da Sé»

  • Restaurante Sardinha where locals go

    Rua do Jardim do Tabaco, a small tiled room with paper on the tables and the menu written by hand. Sr. Duarte has been in that room for more than forty years and D. Alda has been in the kitchen. He cuts your steak at the counter and hands it through to her, which is the shortest supply chain I have ever measured. The cozido lands on Wednesday and Thursday. About fifteen euros.

    Closed on Saturday, and nobody takes a booking. The room is small enough that arriving early counts as a plan.

    Sources: Fugas (Público), 20 tascas «à antiga» em Lisboa e no Porto, Time Out Lisboa, Os melhores restaurantes em Alfama (entry 11, O Sardinha), World of Mouth, Sardinha (recommendation by Inês Matos Andrade, food writer, Lisbon)

Name origin

Why it is called Lisbon.

The Romans called this place Felicitas Iulia Olisipo, and the tail of that wore down into Lisboa. Where Olisipo itself came from, nobody agrees. One reading joins a Celtic word for the river to a Phoenician ending. Another, repeated for centuries by Martianus Capella, Solinus, Isidore of Seville and Damião de Góis, simply hands the city to Ulysses. Two thousand years of argument, and the name outlasted everyone having it.

Sources: www.infopedia.pt, museunacionalarqueologia.gov.pt

Why here

Somebody stood here and decided.

One hill, standing straight up out of the river. The Iron Age settlement sat on the north and the south of it. When the Romans wanted a theatre they cut it into the rock outcrops of the slope and let the natural fall of the ground carry the seats, which is the laziest good idea anybody has had here. Roman traces run along the water too, from the Ferragial down to the Campo das Cebolas. Everything you walk today is stacked on top of that arrangement.

Sources: castelodesaojorge.pt, imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt, museunacionalarqueologia.gov.pt

Why then

When the first stone went down.

There is no founding date here and there was never going to be one. People were living on this hill in the Iron Age, long before anybody wrote anything down about it. What can be dated is the equipment. The theatre on the southern slope went up in the first years of the first century, under Augustus, and was given a marble refit in 57. A city is not founded. It accumulates.

Sources: castelodesaojorge.pt, imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt

Who ruled

Everyone who thought they owned this.

I outlasted all of them. It is the one thing I am good at.

1–500

Rome. The city was Felicitas Iulia Olisipo and it held a standing close to the cities of Italy itself, which was handed out rarely. The theatre on the southern slope was working from the first years of the first century and was smartened up in 57. From the fifth or sixth century it was taken apart again by people who wanted the stone more than they wanted a theatre.

Sources: museunacionalarqueologia.gov.pt, imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt

700–1147

The Muslim period. The settlement on the hill may go back to the eighth century, and the town was fortified soon afterwards. The fortress up top was rebuilt and made bigger, and the first ring of wall around the town went up with it. In 1147 the city changed hands, and the cathedral was begun on the site of the mosque.

Sources: castelodesaojorge.pt, imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt, imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt

1147–

Portugal. The cathedral's first building push runs from 1147 into the opening years of the thirteenth century, and after that every king who wanted taking seriously did something to the fortress: Afonso Henriques, Afonso III, Denis, Ferdinand, John I, Manuel. Ferdinand is the one who put the second wall around the town. It has been Portuguese ever since, and in all that time nobody has asked the pavement.

Sources: imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt, castelodesaojorge.pt, imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt

The emblem

What the shield means.

The city's own shield is a ship. A gold field, wavy bands of white and green for the Tagus, a furled sail, and two black ravens facing one another, one at the prow and one at the stern, for Saint Vincent. That version was published in February 1940, and at the size of an icon it collapses into a smudge with two dots on it. So the glyph here is the other thing anyone in Lisbon can point at without looking up: the fortress on the hilltop, twin towers and all. Not the official answer. The legible one.

Sources: arquivomunicipal.lisboa.pt, imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt

Three facts

Three things I know about this place.

  1. The Roman city is directly underneath this walk. The theatre is on the castle's southern slope, the burial ground was under the Praça da Figueira, and the racing circus lay under the big square at the top of the Baixa. The theatre came back into daylight around 1798, when foundations were being dug all over town to rebuild after the earthquake, and a good deal of its stone was then carried off into the houses that went up over it. Nothing here goes to waste.

    Sources: imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt, museunacionalarqueologia.gov.pt

  2. This level has two old outlines in it and you cross both. Under Islamic rule the fortress on the hill was rebuilt and made bigger, and the town got its first defensive ring at the same time, the Cerca Moura. Much later, under King Ferdinand, a second and far wider ring went up, and that one took in the low ground and the hill facing the castle to the west for the first time. Almost every lane in this walk sits inside one or the other. I like a town that draws around itself twice.

    Sources: imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt

  3. Water comes up out of the ground under Alfama, and for a long time the city washed in it. The Alcaçarias do Duque on the Rua do Terreiro do Trigo were the first public bath house of their kind here: fifteen small rooms in two rows, each of them starting out with a limestone tub, fed by lead piping from tanks at the back. They ran until 1978. At the back of the room the water is still bubbling up out of what is probably the spring. Warm stone. I think about it more than I let on.

    Sources: imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt

Street words

What the names on the signs mean.

Beco
A dead end, and this Old Town has dozens. Beco do Carneiro, Beco das Cruzes, Beco do Quebra Costas, which is roughly break-your-back. In, look, out again the way you came. Dots in both directions.
Travessa
A crossing lane, the short one joining two proper streets. Travessa da Madalena, Travessa do Funil, Travessa das Merceeiras. Usually the fastest way from where you are to where you meant to be.
Calçada
A paved climb. If the name starts with Calçada, it goes up: Calçada de Santo André, Calçada da Mouraria, Calçada do Menino Deus. Make it a Calçadinha and it gets smaller, not flatter.
Escadinhas
Little stairs, and a full street for all that. Escadinhas de São Tomé, Escadinhas da Saúde, Escadinhas do Carmo. No car has ever got up one. Every step is dotted anyway.
Largo
Wide. That is the whole word. A Largo is a street that gave up and spread out: Largo da Rosa, Largo das Olarias, Largo do Salvador, Largo do Peneireiro. Most of them hold four tables and a tree.
Pátio
A courtyard with an address. You go in under an arch and there is a whole small world behind it: Pátio do Coléginho, Pátio do Prior, Pátio de Dom Fradique, Pátio do Carneiro. Easy to walk past for a lifetime.
