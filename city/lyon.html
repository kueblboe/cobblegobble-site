Landmarks

What I would eat first.

Landmarks are fruit. The bigger the sight, the bigger the bonus.

  • Cathédrale Saint-Jean 1,500 points

    Get close to the west face before you look up. The three doorways are framed by hundreds of little carved panels at head height, and by no means all of them are pious. Inside there is a clock that does something on a timetable. I am not telling you what, or when.

  • Église Saint-Nizier 500 points

    Its two towers do not match, and once you have seen that you cannot unsee it. Shops are packed tight on all four sides, so you will meet it from four different lanes and never get the whole of it at once. Go all the way round the outside before you go in.

  • Fontaine Bartholdi 500 points

    Four horses coming at you out of the water, a woman driving them, and nobody involved is slowing down. Mist blows from the horses' nostrils. The thing was designed for a different town that decided it cost too much, and Lyon took it. Stand in front of the horses, not beside them.

  • Place Bellecour 500 points

    Everybody photographs the big one. Off to a corner, far smaller and easy to walk past, a writer sits with the boy he invented. He was born a few streets from where you are. The rest of it is width, and width is rare on this level.

  • Cour des Voraces 250 points

    You do not reach this one across a square. You step in off the street, go through a house, and come out a level further up the slope. No ticket, no gate, and very easy to walk straight past the door. Once you are inside, look up.

  • Église Saint-Georges 250 points

    The downstream end of the level, right on the water, and the quietest corner in it. Come over on the footbridge instead of along the bank: the whole thing was put up to be seen from out on the river. Then turn round and look back at where you have been.

  • Place des Célestins 250 points

    Small, with one very grand front along a side of it. The best thing here is not at eye level and it is not up above you either. There is a single spot in the paving to look from. You can find it.

  • Place Louis Pradel 250 points

    The north end, a terrace lifted above the traffic, with the opera house over it under a long black barrel of a roof. Half the lanes up here drain into it. Sit on the edge a minute and watch which direction everybody arrives from.

  • Pont de la Feuillée 250 points

    Stop halfway across. Look one way, then the other: the Saône with a hill standing over each end of it, which is this whole town in two glances. Then keep going, because nothing lives underneath this particular bit of road and I find that restful.

Name origin

Why it is called Lyon.

Lugdunum. The back half is Gaulish and nobody argues about it: dunum is a hill, or a fort on top of one. The front half is argued about constantly. The museum that stands on that very hill reads Lug as light and calls the name the hill of light, turned to face the sunrise. An older story says the founders watched crows come down here and named the ground after the birds. Either way, somebody looked at a lump of rock and decided it was worth a word.

Sources: lugdunum.grandlyon.com, odysseum.eduscol.education.fr

Why here

Somebody stood here and decided.

Not here. The Romans built up on Fourvière, the hill on the far bank, because from there you look down on two navigable rivers meeting and everything moving between the Mediterranean and the north has to pass underneath you. What you walk in this level is the ground they came down to afterwards, and a good part of it is the Presqu'île, which was filled in and levelled while the town on the hill was still busy. I have a soft spot for made ground. Somebody wanted a street badly enough to build the floor first.

Sources: www.gadagne-lyon.fr p. 1, lugdunum.grandlyon.com, www.gadagne-lyon.fr p. 2

Why then

When the first stone went down.

43 BC, and the reason for it happened somewhere else. Caesar had taken Gaul, Caesar was a year dead, and the Roman settlers pushed out of Vienne needed somewhere to set Rome's authority back down. So a colony went up on the hill, carrying the name of the man sent to found it: Colonia Copia Felix Munatia Lugdunum. That is a great deal of name for a timber fort behind a ditch and a palisade. Sixteen years later Augustus made it the capital of the Gauls and the roads started radiating out from here.

Sources: www.gadagne-lyon.fr p. 1

Who ruled

Everyone who thought they owned this.

I outlasted all of them. It is the one thing I am good at.

-43–461

Rome. The colony went up in 43 BC and sixteen years later Augustus made it the capital of the Gauls, which is as high as anybody has ever placed this town. At its best it held some thirty-five thousand people and had spread down off the hill onto the right bank of the Saône and out across the flat. Then in 297 the job went to Trier, nearer the frontier that mattered by then, and through the third and fourth centuries the hill emptied out. By 461 the city was Burgundian.

Sources: www.gadagne-lyon.fr p. 1, www.gadagne-lyon.fr p. 2

461–558

Burgundians, then Franks. The western empire formally stopped on 4 September 476 and Roman Lugdunum stopped with it; after that Constantinople held a theory about this place and nothing more. The Franks swallowed the Burgundian kingdom in 534, and by 558 the city sat inside Chlothar's. Three sets of owners inside a hundred years, and not one of them moved a street.

Sources: www.gadagne-lyon.fr p. 2

1157–1312

The archbishop, with the paperwork to prove it. On 18 November 1157 the emperor Frederick Barbarossa handed Héracle de Montboissier the regalian rights over Lyon and the country behind it, which made the man inside the cathedral the lord of everything outside it as well. That arrangement ran for close to a hundred and fifty years. Then the archbishops had to give way to Philippe le Bel, king of France, somewhere between 1307 and 1312.

Sources: www.gadagne-lyon.fr

1312–

France, and the townspeople underneath it. Once the king held the jurisdiction, the archbishop Pierre de Savoie granted the citizens their charter on 21 June 1320: they could elect consuls to run themselves, raise their own taxes within limits, keep their own archive, stand their own night watch and hold the keys to the town gates. That archive is still open. It is the reason I can tell you any of this, and it is why I am fairly sure nobody wrote down a single word about me.

Sources: www.archives-lyon.fr, www.gadagne-lyon.fr

The emblem

What the shield means.

Lyon's arms are a silver lion on red, under a blue band carrying three gold lilies. The lion is a pun. This city had its name, and its name meant a hill, centuries before anyone thought of putting an animal on a shield for it. At icon size a lion is also just a lion. So the glyph is a building instead: the west face of the cathedral over the water, seen straight on and flattened to its outline, five uprights stepping up to the tall one in the middle, the whole thing set on one long step.

Sources: fr.wikipedia.org, lugdunum.grandlyon.com, www.lyon.fr, pop.culture.gouv.fr

Three facts

Three things I know about this place.

  1. This level is on the wrong hill, and that is the whole point of it. The Roman town sat up on Fourvière. Through the third and fourth centuries it emptied out and everybody came down: to the right bank of the Saône, and onto the Canabae, the strip of the Presqu'île between Ainay and the Terreaux that had been filled in and levelled during the first century. So a good part of what you are walking here was made on purpose, before there was anything on it worth walking to.

    Sources: www.gadagne-lyon.fr p. 2

  2. From the last quarter of the fifteenth century the presses here made Lyon one of the capitals of the book, behind Venice and Paris, and four trade fairs a year brought the buyers in to empty them. The workshops crowded around a single street, the rue Mercière, which is on your route. There is a passage on this level still named after the printers, and the museum of printing is a few doors off it, in a Renaissance house on the rue de la Poulaillerie. That address is the second reason I like this trade.

    Sources: numelyo.bm-lyon.fr, www.lyon.fr

  3. In 1528 a cloth merchant on the slopes of the Croix-Rousse turned up two broken pieces of a bronze sheet. Engraved on it, word for word, was a speech the emperor Claudius made to the senate in Rome in the year 48, arguing that the leading men of Gaul should be allowed to sit in it. Two hundred and twenty-two kilos of bronze. The city bought it in 1530 for fifty-eight gold écus and hung it in the town house, and there is a street up on that slope still carrying its name.

    Sources: lugdunum.grandlyon.com, www.gadagne-lyon.fr p. 2, www.hisoma.mom.fr

Street words

What the names on the signs mean.

Montée
A climb that counts as a street. Montée de la Grande Côte, Montée des Carmélites, Montée du Garillan, Montée du Perron, Montée Saint-Sébastien. Lyon has two hills and has never once pretended otherwise.
Traboule
A Lyon word for a Lyon habit: a passage cutting through a building from one street to the next. Longue Traboule, Traboule du Gouverneur. You go in at a door and come out somewhere else entirely, which is the most useful thing a street has ever done for me.
Cour
A courtyard the map counts as a street. Cour des Moirages, Cour du Midi, Cour Saint-Henri, Cour Sainte-Élisabeth. There are dots in there, so in you go.
Passerelle
A footbridge. Passerelle Saint Georges, Passerelle Saint Vincent, Passerelle Pierre Truche. Narrow, over water, no cars on it. Three of my favourite conditions at the same time.
Impasse
A dead end. Impasse de la Pêcherie, Impasse de la Platière, Impasse des Capucins, Impasse Saint-Polycarpe. In, and then out again the same way. I have made my peace with these.
-erie
The ending a trade leaves behind on a street. Rue de la Poulaillerie sold poultry, Rue de la Fromagerie cheese, Impasse de la Pêcherie fish, Rue Lainerie wool. Three of those four are lunch, which tells you what streets are really for.
